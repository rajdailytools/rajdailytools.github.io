Understanding the US Tax System

A complete guide to understanding your salary, taxes, and take-home pay in the USA.

What is the US Salary Tax System?

The United States uses a progressive federal income tax system, meaning the more you earn, the higher percentage you pay on each additional dollar. However, you are NOT taxed at a single flat rate — instead, your income is taxed at different rates for each bracket it falls into. This is a common source of confusion for newcomers and international workers.

For example, if you earn $80,000 annually as a single filer in 2026, you don't pay 22% on the entire amount. You pay 10% on the first bracket, 12% on the next, and 22% only on income above the third bracket threshold. This is called the marginal tax rate system.

Federal Tax vs. State Tax — What's the Difference?

Every American (and US worker) pays federal income tax, which goes to the national government. On top of this, most states also charge their own income tax. Here's a simplified breakdown:

  • Federal Tax: Applies to all US income earners. Rates range from 10% to 37% in progressive brackets.
  • State Tax: Varies by state. Nine states have zero income tax (Texas, Florida, Nevada, Alaska, Washington, Wyoming, South Dakota, Tennessee, New Hampshire). Hawaii tops the list at 11%+.
  • FICA (Social Security + Medicare): Additional ~7.65% on top of income taxes — not included in simplified tools but worth knowing.

Why Does Your Salary Look High But Take-Home is Less?

This is the most common shock for new workers in the USA. A $100,000 salary sounds like $8,333/month, but your actual take-home might be closer to $5,500–$6,500 depending on your state and filing status. The difference goes to federal tax, state tax, Social Security, Medicare, health insurance, and retirement contributions.

For example, in California — one of the highest-tax states — a $100,000 salary single filer could take home around $65,000–$68,000 after all taxes. In Texas (no state tax), that same earner might take home $72,000–$75,000.

Real-Life Salary Examples (2026)

Here are some quick estimates to help you benchmark your situation (approximate, for reference only):

  • $50,000 in Texas (Single): ~$38,000–$40,000 take-home (no state tax)
  • $75,000 in New York (Single): ~$53,000–$56,000 take-home
  • $100,000 in California (Single): ~$64,000–$68,000 take-home
  • $150,000 in Florida (Married): ~$108,000–$112,000 take-home

Common Mistakes People Make

  • Assuming the salary offer is what they'll receive in hand — it's not.
  • Not accounting for state taxes when comparing job offers across states.
  • Ignoring the impact of filing status — married couples can save significantly by filing jointly.
  • Forgetting pre-tax benefits like 401k or HSA contributions that reduce taxable income.
  • Not planning for quarterly estimated taxes if self-employed or freelancing.

Why Calculating Your Net Salary is Important

Knowing your take-home salary helps you plan your budget, negotiate job offers, compare states for relocation, and set financial goals accurately. A job paying $90,000 in Austin, Texas may leave you with more money than a $100,000 job in San Francisco after rent, taxes, and cost of living are factored in. ❓ FAQs

Frequently Asked Questions

How much tax is deducted from salary in the USA?+
It depends on your income and state. Federal tax ranges from 10% to 37%. State tax ranges from 0% (Texas, Florida) to 11%+ (Hawaii). On average, most middle-income earners pay 20–30% in combined federal and state taxes.
Which US states have no income tax?+
Nine states have zero state income tax: Texas, Florida, Nevada, Alaska, Wyoming, South Dakota, Washington, Tennessee, and New Hampshire. If you live or work in these states, you only pay federal tax on your income.
What is take-home salary?+
Take-home salary (also called net salary) is the amount you actually receive in your bank account after all taxes, deductions, and contributions are subtracted from your gross salary. It's your real spendable income.
Is this US salary calculator accurate?+
This tool provides highly reliable estimates based on 2026 federal tax brackets and approximate state tax rates. However, it does not include FICA taxes (Social Security + Medicare ~7.65%), local city taxes, or all deductions. Always verify with a tax professional or the IRS website for precise figures.
What is federal income tax?+
Federal income tax is a tax collected by the US federal government on your earnings. It uses a progressive bracket system — you pay 10% on the first portion of income, 12% on the next, 22% on the next, and so on up to 37% for the highest earners. The rate you're in is your "marginal rate."
What is state income tax?+
State income tax is an additional tax collected by your state government. It varies widely — from 0% in states like Texas and Florida, to flat rates (e.g., Illinois at 4.95%), to progressive systems (e.g., California up to ~13.3%). It is paid on top of federal income tax.
Does filing status affect my tax?+
Yes, significantly. Married couples filing jointly get wider tax brackets and a higher standard deduction, which reduces taxable income. Single filers pay taxes at comparatively tighter brackets. For the same income, a married couple typically pays less tax than a single person.
What are additional deductions I can claim?+
Common pre-tax deductions that reduce your taxable income include: 401(k) retirement contributions, HSA/FSA contributions, employer-sponsored health insurance premiums, student loan interest, and business expenses (for self-employed). Enter these in the "Additional Deductions" field for a more accurate result.
